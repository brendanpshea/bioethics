# Bioethics — Essay Questions

There is **one question for each of the twelve case studies**. In each essay session you will be
given **one** case, drawn from the chapters that exam covers:

| Session | Cases you might be given |
|---|---|
| **Exam 1** | 1 Dax Cowart · 2 Tuskegee & Guatemala · 3 Lesné & Aβ\*56 · 4 Kate Cox & Karsan |
| **Exam 2** | 5 Adriana Smith · 6 He Jiankui · 7 Noelia Castillo · 8 Singapore |
| **Exam 3** | 9 Wakefield & MMR · 10 Optum · 11 *Skrmetti* · 12 David Bennett |

You will not know which one you are getting, so work through all four. **The case text will be on
your screen** — you are not being tested on recall.

## The basics, in one box

> **75-minute session. About 60 minutes of writing. 400–600 words. Five paragraphs.**
>
> You may label your paragraphs (`¶1 — My position`). It helps.
>
> **Explain it like I'm 12.** Every question below marks the terms you need to define in plain words
> the first time you use them. If you can't explain an idea simply, you don't understand it yet.
>
> **¶5 is where you come in.** Every question ends by asking you to connect the case to yourself. Use
> **any one** of these — they are worth the same credit:
>
> 1. Something that happened to you or someone in your family.
> 2. Something you have seen at work, in clinical, or in a placement.
> 3. Something a friend, a neighbor, or someone in your community went through.
> 4. A story you followed closely in the news.
> 5. **Put yourself in it.** Imagine the case were about you, or someone you love.
>
> **You never have to share anything private** — option 5 earns full marks. If you write about a real
> patient, remove anything that could identify them.

*Full guidance, sentence frames, and a worked example are in **How to Write a Bioethics Essay**.*

## 1 — Dax Cowart
*Paired with: History & the Clinical Relationship (Ch 2)*

Cowart was burned over two-thirds of his body, blinded, and demanded for more than a year that
treatment stop. He was overruled. He survived, went to law school, built a career — and said for the
rest of his life that his refusal should have been honored.

> **Question: Does the fact that he was later glad to be alive settle whether the team was right to
> override him?**

- **¶1 — Your position.** Say whether his later gladness settles it. One sentence, first sentence.
- **¶2 — The case.** The conflict is between his right to refuse treatment on his own body and the
  team's duty to save a patient they could save. Include **one specific detail** — how long he
  refused, what the treatments involved, or something he said.
- **¶3 — The class material.** Use **The Autonomy Argument** and **The Retrospective-Endorsement
  Objection**. The objection is about whether a *right* depends on how things *turn out*. Say whether
  you think it does, and why.
  - 🔍 **Explain like I'm 12:** *autonomy*, *competent / capacity*.
- **¶4 — The other side.** The strongest objection is **The Beneficence / Temporary-Incapacity
  Reply** — that pain that severe undermines a person's capacity to refuse. Make it sound reasonable
  before you answer it.
- **¶5 — You.** Think of a time you, or someone close to you, made a decision about their own body or
  health that other people thought was wrong. Did being right or wrong *later* change who should have
  made the call at the time? (Or use option 5 and put yourself in his room.)

**Cross-chapter connection:** Chapter 1 gave you four principles. Which two collide hardest here —
and does naming the collision actually tell you which should win?

## 2 — Tuskegee & Guatemala
*Paired with: Research Ethics (Ch 3)*

Tuskegee ran for forty years. Doctors and nurses watched it happen. Some of them knew it was wrong.

> **Question: Would today's rules have stopped it?**

- **¶1 — Your position.** Yes or no. One sentence.
- **¶2 — The case.** The conflict is between two explanations of the failure: missing *rules* versus
  missing *courage*. Include **one specific detail** — how long it ran, what subjects were told, or
  what happened when penicillin became available.
- **¶3 — The class material.** Use **The Regulatory-Gap Argument** and **The Conscience Argument**.
  One says the system lacked safeguards; the other says people who knew better did not refuse. Which
  better explains forty years?
  - 🔍 **Explain like I'm 12:** *informed consent*, *IRB (institutional review board)*.
- **¶4 — The other side.** The strongest objection is **The Institutional-Design Reply** — that
  relying on individual conscience is exactly what failed, and rules are what fixed it.
- **¶5 — You.** Think of a time you saw something wrong where you worked, studied, or volunteered.
  Did you say something? What made that easy or hard — the rules, the people, or what it would have
  cost you? Connect it to whether rules or conscience is the better safeguard.

**Cross-chapter connection:** Chapter 2 gave you the Belmont principles. Pick the one Tuskegee
violated *most*, and defend your choice against the obvious rival.

## 3 — Sylvain Lesné and the Aβ\*56 Affair
*Paired with: Research Ethics (Ch 3)*

Images in a landmark Alzheimer's paper were manipulated. Hundreds of papers cited the work. Enormous
funding followed the amyloid hypothesis.

> **Question: Who was wronged by this fraud — and is weak-but-honest science a *different* kind of
> problem, or the same kind?**

- **¶1 — Your position.** Name who was wronged most, and say whether honest-but-weak science belongs
  in the same category. One sentence.
- **¶2 — The case.** The conflict is between fraud as an individual crime and fraud as a symptom of
  how the field was already working. Include **one specific detail** — the manipulated images, the
  citation count, or the cost of the drugs that followed.
- **¶3 — The class material.** Use **Schrag's Argument** and **The "Robustness" Reply** — the claim
  that the field's conclusions never really rested on the faked images. Name the premise they
  disagree about.
  - 🔍 **Explain like I'm 12:** *replication*, *peer review*, *the amyloid hypothesis*.
- **¶4 — The other side.** Bring in **The Medical Nihilism Argument** or **The Gentle Medicine
  Argument**. If a field absorbed fraud that smoothly, what does that say about the rest of its
  evidence — and is that a reason to trust medicine less, or to expect less of it?
- **¶5 — You.** Think of a time you trusted an expert, a doctor, or an official source and later had
  reason to doubt it. What would it have taken to restore your trust? Tie that to what you said in ¶1
  about who was wronged.

**Cross-chapter connection:** Chapter 3 taught clinical equipoise. If the evidence base was weaker
than advertised, were the trials built on it ever in genuine equipoise?

## 4 — Kate Cox and Damla Karsan
*Paired with: Abortion (Ch 4)*

Conscience protections in medicine were built for the clinician who **refuses**. Karsan wanted to
**provide** — and put her name on a lawsuit to do it.

> **Question: Should conscientious provision get the same protection as conscientious refusal?**

- **¶1 — Your position.** Yes or no. One sentence.
- **¶2 — The case.** The conflict is between a physician's professional judgment and a criminal
  statute. Include **one specific detail** — what Karsan risked, what the Texas exception required,
  or what the court said about "reasonable medical judgment."
- **¶3 — The class material.** Use **The Integrity Argument for Refusal** and **The Argument for
  Conscientious Provision**. Both appeal to professional integrity. Say exactly where they part
  company.
  - 🔍 **Explain like I'm 12:** *conscientious refusal*, *conscientious provision*, *professional
    integrity*.
- **¶4 — The other side.** The strongest objection is **The Stahl-Emanuel Reply**: refusal asks only
  to be left out, while provision asks the profession to underwrite breaking the law. State it in its
  most forceful form before you answer it.
- **¶5 — You.** Think of a time your job, your training, or your role required you to do something you
  thought was wrong — or stopped you from doing something you thought was right. What did you do, and
  what did it cost? *This case touches on abortion; if you would rather not write from personal
  experience here, use option 5 and reason about the situation from the outside. It is worth the same
  credit.*

**Cross-chapter connection:** Chapter 2 described models of the clinical relationship. Which model is
Karsan practicing when she puts her name on the lawsuit?

## 5 — Adriana Smith
*Paired with: Autonomy, Paternalism & Reproduction (Ch 5)*

Smith was legally dead. Her body was maintained on machines for about four months over her family's
objection. The state Attorney General said the law did not require it. The hospital continued anyway.

> **Question: What kind of medical act was the hospital performing for those four months?**

- **¶1 — Your position.** Name it — and say whether it was justified. One sentence.
- **¶2 — The case.** The conflict is between a family's authority over a dead relative's body and an
  institution's reading of a fetal-personhood law. Include **one specific detail** — the four months,
  what her mother said, or the Attorney General's statement.
- **¶3 — The class material.** Use **The "Not an Abortion" Argument** and **The Experimentation
  Argument (Caplan)**. Caplan's claim is that this fit *none* of medicine's existing categories —
  not treatment, not organ donation, not research. Is he right?
  - 🔍 **Explain like I'm 12:** *brain death*, *fetal personhood*.
- **¶4 — The other side.** Answer the strongest objection to your ¶1. If you said the hospital acted
  wrongly, the objection is that a fetus with a real chance of life was at stake and caution was
  owed. If you said it acted rightly, the objection is that nobody consented to any of it.
- **¶5 — You.** Who would you want making decisions about your body if you could not? Have you ever
  told them? If you have watched a family and a hospital disagree about someone who could not speak,
  use that instead. *You are free to keep this general.*

**Cross-chapter connection:** Chapter 3 distinguished research from care. If this was neither, what
protections did Smith and her family lose by falling outside both?

## 6 — He Jiankui and the CRISPR Twins
*Paired with: Genomics & Enhancement (Ch 6)*

Two children were born with edited genomes. The stated goal was resistance to HIV. He went to prison
for three years.

> **Question: Was the deepest wrong here the *risk* he took, or the *consent* he failed to get?**

- **¶1 — Your position.** Risk or consent. One sentence.
- **¶2 — The case.** The conflict is between a scientist's judgment that a benefit justified an
  experiment and the fact that the subjects could never agree to it. Include **one specific detail** —
  what the parents were told, the secrecy, or what happened to the third child.
- **¶3 — The class material.** Use **He Jiankui's Defense** and **The Risk-Benefit Reply**. Be fair to
  the defense — he did believe he was preventing a real disease.
  - 🔍 **Explain like I'm 12:** *CRISPR*, *germline vs. somatic editing*.
- **¶4 — The other side.** Bring in **The Future Person's Consent Reply**. Nobody can consent for a
  person who does not yet exist. Does that make germline editing uniquely wrong — or is it true of
  every reproductive decision any parent has ever made?
- **¶5 — You.** Think of a significant decision someone made *for* you before you were old enough to
  object — medical, religious, educational, where you lived. How do you feel about it now, and does
  that change what you said in ¶1 about consent?

**Cross-chapter connection:** Chapter 3 listed the elements of valid consent. Which element failed
most completely with the parents — and would fixing it have made the experiment acceptable?

## 7 — Noelia Castillo
*Paired with: End-of-Life Care (Ch 7)*

This case reverses the usual fight. In Quinlan, Cruzan, and Schiavo, families fought to stop treatment
while the state insisted on life. Here a competent woman fought for death while her father fought to
keep her alive — for 601 days.

> **Question: When a competent patient and her family want opposite things, how much weight should the
> family's objection carry?**

- **¶1 — Your position.** State your rule in one sentence: "The family's objection should ______."
- **¶2 — The case.** The conflict is between a patient the courts repeatedly found competent and a
  father who believed her illness was making the decision for her. Include **one specific detail** —
  the 601 days, the courts that ruled, or something she said in her final interview.
- **¶3 — The class material.** Use **The Autonomy Argument** and **The Capacity Challenge**. Explain
  what *capacity* means in this course — and why it is the hinge the whole dispute turns on.
  - 🔍 **Explain like I'm 12:** *capacity*, *euthanasia vs. assisted death*, *borderline personality
    disorder*.
- **¶4 — The other side.** Bring in whichever cuts against your ¶1 — **The Vulnerability / Disability
  Argument** or **The Anti–Double-Standard Reply**. Remember her father was not a stranger, and he
  could have been right.
- **¶5 — You.** Have you ever seen someone's judgment discounted because of a mental-health
  diagnosis — at work, in a family, or in your own life? What would it take to convince you that a
  person in serious distress was still deciding for themselves? *This case involves suicide and
  psychiatric illness. If writing from experience is not something you want to do here, use option 5.
  It is worth the same credit.*

**Cross-chapter connection:** Chapter 5A separated **soft** paternalism (overriding a *compromised*
choice) from **hard** paternalism (overriding a *competent* one). Which was her father asking the
courts to perform?

## 8 — Singapore's Health Care System
*Paired with: Justice & Health Care Allocation (Ch 8)*

Singapore spends about a quarter of what the US spends per person and beats it on most headline
measures. It also excludes a large migrant workforce from its subsidies.

> **Question: Can the United States learn from Singapore, or is the result inseparable from the
> trade-offs that produce it?**

- **¶1 — Your position.** Can it, can't it, or can only partly. One sentence.
- **¶2 — The case.** The conflict is between admiring an outcome and importing the machinery that
  produced it. Include **one specific detail** — the spending gap, the life-expectancy gap, or the
  share paid out of pocket.
- **¶3 — The class material.** Use **The Emulation Argument** and **The Whole-System Argument**. The
  second says you cannot lift one piece out of a system that only works as a whole. Name the premise
  they disagree about.
  - 🔍 **Explain like I'm 12:** *out-of-pocket cost*, *forced savings (MediSave)*, *single-payer*.
- **¶4 — The other side.** Bring in **The Exclusion Argument**. If the headline outcomes depend partly
  on who is left out of the count, what happens to the comparison?
- **¶5 — You.** Think about a time cost shaped a health decision for you or someone in your family —
  a bill, a deductible, a prescription not filled, a visit put off. Would Singapore's design have
  helped or hurt in that situation?

**Cross-chapter connection:** Chapter 8A gave you theories of justice. Pick the one Singapore's design
best serves, and the one it most clearly violates.

## 9 — Andrew Wakefield and the MMR-Autism Myth
*Paired with: Public Health Ethics (Ch 9)*

The science was settled decades ago. The paper was retracted and the author lost his license. The
belief spread anyway — and measles came back.

> **Question: When the facts are settled and the fear spreads regardless, what may a free society do
> about it — and what may it never do?**

- **¶1 — Your position.** State your rule in one sentence.
- **¶2 — The case.** The conflict is between protecting a population and respecting people who do not
  believe you. Include **one specific detail** — the retraction, the fall in vaccination rates, or a
  recent outbreak.
- **¶3 — The class material.** Use **The Correction Argument** and **The Backfire Reply**. Does telling
  people they are wrong make things better or worse?
  - 🔍 **Explain like I'm 12:** *herd immunity*, *retraction*, *vaccine hesitancy*.
- **¶4 — The other side.** Bring in **The Earned-Distrust Argument** or **The Engagement Argument**.
  The hardest version of the other side is that some distrust of medicine was *earned* — and Case 2 is
  the receipt.
- **¶5 — You.** Think of a time you, or someone close to you, believed something about health that
  turned out to be false. What changed it — or what stopped it from changing? Be honest about whether
  being corrected by an authority would have worked.

**Cross-chapter connection:** Chapter 2 and Case 2 are about how trust was broken. Does a history like
Tuskegee change what public health is *permitted* to do now, or only what is likely to work?

## 10 — The Optum Care-Management Algorithm
*Paired with: AI in Medicine (Ch 10)*

The algorithm used health *cost* to predict health *need*. Because less had historically been spent on
Black patients, it judged them healthier than they were — and sent care elsewhere.

> **Question: Who is responsible for this harm — the builders, the hospitals that bought it, or the
> clinicians who trusted it?**

- **¶1 — Your position.** Name who bears it. One sentence.
- **¶2 — The case.** The conflict is that no one involved intended to discriminate and the system
  discriminated anyway. Include **one specific detail** — the target variable, or the size of the
  disparity the audit found.
- **¶3 — The class material.** Use **The Colorblind Accuracy Argument** and **The Warped Proxy
  Argument**. The algorithm never saw race. Explain, in plain words, how it discriminated anyway.
  - 🔍 **Explain like I'm 12:** *algorithm*, *proxy variable*, *bias*.
- **¶4 — The other side.** Bring in **The Fixable Machine Argument** — the optimistic view that this
  bias was found and corrected, which is more than we can say for bias inside a human head. Then
  answer it, using **The Vanishing Label Argument** if it helps.
- **¶5 — You.** Think of a time a score, a form, or an automated system made a decision about you — a
  credit check, an insurance rate, a job screening, a triage tool. Could you find out why? Could you
  argue with it? Connect that to who you blamed in ¶1.

**Cross-chapter connection:** Chapter 8 was about justice in allocation. Was this a *new* injustice, or
an old one automated and scaled?

## 11 — *United States v. Skrmetti*
*Paired with: Gender, Identity & Disability (Ch 11)*

The same drug is legal for one purpose and illegal for another. Whether that is a line about
**purpose** or a line about **sex** decided the case.

> **Question: Is SB1 a classification by purpose, or a classification by sex?**

- **¶1 — Your position.** Purpose or sex. One sentence.
- **¶2 — The case.** The conflict is that both sides agree on what the statute says and disagree about
  what kind of line it draws. Include **one specific detail** — the precocious-puberty comparison, or
  what the majority said about legislatures.
- **¶3 — The class material.** Use **The Sex-Classification Argument** and **The Medical-Purpose
  Reply**. Name precisely what they disagree about — it is narrower than it first looks.
  - 🔍 **Explain like I'm 12:** *equal protection*, *levels of scrutiny*, *gender dysphoria*.
- **¶4 — The other side.** The majority held that where medical evidence is contested, *legislatures*
  rather than courts should decide. Answer that — or, if it is your position, answer the objection
  that weak evidence is a reason for courts to be *more* protective of an individual, not less.
- **¶5 — You.** Think of a decision about your own health or body that someone else made because of
  your age — a parent, a doctor, a school. What would you have wanted, and who should have had the
  final say? *If you would rather not write from experience on this case, use option 5.*

**Cross-chapter connection:** Chapter 5A was about paternalism and Chapter 2 about who decides for a
minor. Whose autonomy is actually at stake here — the teenager's, or the parents'?

## 12 — David Bennett and the Pig-Heart Transplant
*Paired with: Animal Ethics (Ch 12)*

Bennett was dying and out of options. He consented to an organ no human being had ever received.

> **Question: Was his consent genuinely free — and does it matter?**

- **¶1 — Your position.** Free or not, and whether that settles anything. One sentence.
- **¶2 — The case.** The conflict is between respecting a dying man's choice and admitting he had no
  real alternative to choose from. Include **one specific detail** — his condition, how long he lived,
  or what he was told about the risks.
- **¶3 — The class material.** Use **The Rule-of-Rescue Argument** and **The Therapeutic-Misconception
  Reply**. When someone has no alternative, is agreement a choice or the absence of one?
  - 🔍 **Explain like I'm 12:** *xenotransplantation*, *therapeutic misconception*.
- **¶4 — The other side.** Bring in **The Public-Risk Argument**. The infection risk of a
  cross-species transplant falls on people who were never in the consent conversation. Can any
  individual's consent settle that?
- **¶5 — You.** Think of a time you agreed to something because you had no real alternative — a job, a
  lease, a treatment, a form you had to sign. Was that consent? Connect your answer to what you said
  about Bennett in ¶1.

**Cross-chapter connection:** Chapter 3 separated research from care. Bennett's surgery was a rescue;
the field has since moved into regulated clinical trials. Does that shift make consent easier to
obtain honestly, or harder?

## Preparing

You cannot prepare by memorizing an essay — you will not know which of the four cases you are given,
and a pre-written answer to the wrong question earns nothing.

For each case in your exam's range, work through this once:

1. Find the case's named arguments. Every case has them.
2. Work out **what premise the two sides actually disagree about**. That one sentence is your ¶3.
3. Decide which side you are on, then write down the best objection to your own view. That is ¶4.
4. Practice defining the "explain like I'm 12" terms out loud, in plain words, without notes.
5. Decide which rung of the ladder you would use for ¶5, and why it fits your position.

Four ten-minute plans per exam. That is the whole job.
