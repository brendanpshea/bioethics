Part B: Engineering Persons — CRISPR, Enhancement, and Identity

An Introduction to Bioethics.

Would You Select?

You and your partner are doing IVF. Six embryos make it to the testing stage. The clinic offers a new add-on for $50,000. They will read each embryo’s whole genome and rank them on predicted IQ, height, and risk for common diseases. You can pick the embryo at the top of the list. The doctor stresses that it is legal, voluntary, and currently used by other couples in the clinic.

Write or discuss:

  1. Is this medical treatment, disease prevention, or something else? Does the label matter?
  2. The expected IQ gain from picking the top embryo is about 2 points. Does that change your answer?
  3. If you would not pay $50,000 for this, but it became free tomorrow, would you take it then?

This scenario is not hypothetical. US fertility clinics began offering whole-embryo polygenic scoring around 2020, and the companies selling it appear later in this chapter.

After You’ve Discussed

Note

  • Treatment restores normal function — an antibiotic for an infection, insulin for diabetes.
  • Prevention keeps you out of harm — a vaccine, a statin.
  • Enhancement improves you beyond the normal range — sharper memory, taller body, longer life.

The Treatment-Enhancement Line

Why This Line Matters in Practice

The line between treatment and enhancement does work in three places.

  • Insurance. Health insurers pay for treatment. They do not pay for enhancement. Whether a procedure is one or the other decides who can afford it.
  • Professional duty. Doctors have an obligation to treat. They do not have an obligation to enhance. A patient cannot demand enhancement the way she can demand treatment.
  • Regulation. The FDA approves drugs as treatments for specific conditions. Off-label and enhancement uses fall outside its main review.

Move a procedure from one side of the line to the other, and three different systems respond.

Three Accounts of the Line

Account Treatment is… Where it breaks
Professional-domain Whatever the profession agrees to do The profession’s scope grows with demand: shyness became social anxiety disorder, aging became “low T”
Normal-function (Boorse, Daniels) Restoring species-typical function Prevention: a vaccine pushes immunity above normal on purpose, yet is plainly medicine
Disease-based (Gert, Culver, Clouser) Addressing a diagnosable malady Nosological elasticity: disease categories expand to license the interventions people want

Each account does real work in clinical practice, and each has a failure it cannot fix (Moseley and Juengst 2025; Boorse 1977; Gert et al. 2006).

From Somatic to Germline

Somatic vs. Germline

Somatic changes

  • In one tissue, in one person.
  • Not in sperm or egg cells.
  • Not inherited.
  • The change dies with the patient.
  • All currently approved gene therapies are somatic.

Germline changes

  • In every cell of the resulting person, including reproductive cells.
  • Heritable. Passed to children.
  • Each descendant inherits it.
  • Banned for clinical use in nearly every country.
  • The He Jiankui case is the only known human germline edit to date.

The same molecular tool can do either. The moral weight is in which cells it touches.

CRISPR-Cas9 — How It Works

CRISPR-Cas9 is, in plain terms, a programmable find-and-replace for DNA. You design a short guide molecule that matches the sequence you want to change. The guide drags Cas9 (a cutting enzyme) to that spot. Cas9 cuts. The cell tries to repair the cut, and the edit ends up in place.

Casgevy (FDA, December 2023)

  • The first CRISPR therapy approved in the US treats sickle-cell disease, which affects about 100,000 Americans, most of them Black.
  • Doctors harvest the patient’s stem cells, edit them to switch on a different form of hemoglobin, and reinfuse them after weeks of chemotherapy.
  • It is a somatic edit: it changes the patient, not her future children.
  • The list price is about $2.2 million, and Medicaid coverage varies by state. The access question is a justice question.

He Jiankui — Briefly

What he did. In November 2018, Chinese scientist He Jiankui announced he had used CRISPR to edit the CCR5 gene in human embryos. He had implanted the embryos. Twin girls — Lulu and Nana — had been born. A third edited child was born shortly after.

His stated goal was to confer resistance to HIV. The edit was performed without serious clinical justification, with deeply flawed consent, and outside any sanctioned oversight.

He was sentenced to three years in prison in 2019 and released in 2022. The international consensus against clinical germline editing has held so far.

Paired case: He Jiankui’s CRISPR Twins — the full case study, including the consent failures and the scientific community’s response.

Cognitive Enhancement

Off-Label Stimulants in Healthy Adults

  • The most common enhancement today involves a prescription pad, not CRISPR. Roughly 1 in 6 US college students reports nonmedical use of stimulants like Adderall, with higher rates in nursing, medical, and law programs.
  • In diagnosed ADHD, stimulants clearly improve attention.
  • In healthy adults, the effect is small: a modest boost on simple attention tasks and almost none on complex problem-solving. The largest effect is on perceived productivity.
  • Side effects are real: insomnia, anxiety, raised heart rate, dependence.
  • US childhood ADHD diagnoses rose from about 3% in 1997 to about 11% in 2022, as the criteria broadened.

Thought Question — A Roommate’s NCLEX Request

Your roommate, a healthy nursing student with no ADHD diagnosis, asks to borrow one of your Adderall pills the night before NCLEX. She is anxious. She has heard from upperclassmen that “everyone uses it.” You are prescribed it for a diagnosis you have had since high school.

Write or discuss.

  1. Apply the three accounts of the treatment-enhancement line to her request. Which one makes the strongest case for “no”?
  2. Does the answer change if she says she just wants to try one pill at a normal dose?
  3. If you would say no to your roommate, would you say no to yourself using the same drug to gain an edge on the same test?

After You’ve Discussed

Note

The equity angle. If passing NCLEX increasingly requires stimulants, the test starts measuring access to a diagnosis, not nursing knowledge. An enhancement that everyone uses stops being an advantage and becomes a requirement.

The three accounts give three different reasons for “no.” Professional-domain: prescribing is a professional act, and sharing a prescription with someone undiagnosed is outside it. Normal-function: the drug pushes attention above the normal range in a healthy user. Disease-based: she has no malady, so there is nothing to treat.

Who Is Wronged? The Enhancement Debate

Parfit’s 14-Year-Old Girl

Important

The thought experiment

Imagine a 14-year-old girl who is thinking about having a child. If she has the child now, the child will have a difficult start — limited resources, an inexperienced parent, predictable hardships. If she waits until she is 22, she will be more prepared, more stable, and the child she has then will have a much better life.

It seems obvious that waiting is the right thing to do.

But here is the puzzle. The child she would have at 14 is not the same person as the child she would have at 22. Different egg, different sperm, different person. If she waits, the first child never exists. He doesn’t get a better life; he simply does not get a life at all.

— Derek Parfit, Reasons and Persons (1984)

Parfit’s Puzzle

So who was wronged by the early pregnancy?

  • Not the actual child. His alternative was nonexistence. He is glad to be alive. He has no complaint.
  • Not the never-existing child. That child has no complaints, because that child never existed.

We feel a strong intuition that the early pregnancy was wrong. However, the standard person-affecting reasoning cannot say who was wronged. (Parfit 1984)

This matters everywhere we choose the conditions of someone’s existence — embryo selection, germline editing, even decisions about when to conceive.

Savulescu — Procreative Beneficence

The argument. If you are going to bring a child into existence, you have a moral reason to bring the child with the best expected life. The reasoning is straightforward consequentialism: more well-being is better than less well-being, all else equal.

The argument applies the same way to disease risk and to traits. If a parent should pick the embryo less likely to develop cancer, the parent should also pick the embryo more likely to be tall, intelligent, or cheerful — to the extent those traits track well-being.

Savulescu calls this procreative beneficence. It is the most permissive position in the contemporary literature.

(Savulescu 2001)

Sandel’s Giftedness Argument

The argument is not that enhancement makes children worse off. It often does not. The argument is about what enhancement does to the parent and to the practice of parenting.

  • A parent who accepts the child as a gift can love the child unconditionally. Effort goes into the child’s flourishing on the child’s own terms.
  • A parent who designs the child treats the child as a project. Love starts to track how well the project is going.
  • Beyond the family, a society of designed children loses what Sandel calls solidarity — the sense that we are all subject to the same lottery and owe each other something for it.

The argument is not against medicine. It is against the idea that parenting is a manufacturing problem.

Habermas — Making vs. Growing

For Habermas, the wrong of enhancement is not in the child’s outcomes. It is in the kind of being a designed child is.

  • A child who grew — under nature’s contingency — can come to understand her life as her own. The traits she has are not someone else’s choice.
  • A child who was made — selected or edited toward a specific design — is, in a real sense, the project of her parents’ lives. She cannot retroactively consent to that. She cannot un-design herself.

This is not a religious argument. It is instead an argument about the structure of autonomy. The future adult is owed a life that is hers, not an extension of someone else’s plan. (Habermas 2003)

Three Positions at a Glance

Enhancement is A duty, where it improves the child’s life A corrosion of the parent-child relationship A wrong to the future autonomous adult

All three can agree that Casgevy is fine, but split on embryo selection.

Open Future, Now for the Body

  • Part A used Feinberg’s open future for genetic information: don’t tell an eight-year-old her BRCA risk before she can decide whether to know.
  • The engineering version: don’t build her toward a specific future before she can decide what future she wants (Feinberg 1980).
  • This answers the non-identity puzzle for He Jiankui’s twins. They cannot say they were harmed, since the alternative was not existing. The wrong lies in the relationship the experiment set up with two future adults who could never agree to be designed.

Polygenic Embryo Selection

What Polygenic Embryo Selection Is

  • No editing and no CRISPR, only selection among embryos that already exist.
  • Clinics have screened out single-gene diseases (Tay-Sachs, cystic fibrosis) for decades. What is new is selecting on risk of common disease, and on traits like height or predicted IQ.
  • Orchid Health sells whole-genome embryo screening across hundreds of conditions. Heliospect Genomics explicitly scores for IQ. Both operate legally in the US; no federal law forbids it.

How Accurate Is a Polygenic IQ Score?

  • Current polygenic scores for IQ explain about 10–15% of the variance in intelligence-test performance. The rest is environment, gene-environment interaction, and noise.
  • For a couple selecting among 6–10 sibling embryos, the expected IQ gain from picking the top-ranked one is roughly 2–4 points. Within the noise of the test itself.
  • Predictions work best in the population the score was trained on — overwhelmingly people of European ancestry, as Part A noted.
  • Almost everything that makes a person’s life go well — temperament, motivation, the household they grow up in — is not in the score.

The product is a small statistical nudge, not a designer baby.

Back to the Clinic

Return to the six embryos from the start of class. The top embryo’s predicted IQ is 2.3 points above the bottom one, and the counselor says “most of our families choose the top.”

  1. Has your answer changed, now that you know what the score can and cannot do?
  2. Suppose the gap were 10 points instead of 2.3. Does that change it?
  3. If you would not select for IQ, would you select for low risk of type 2 diabetes? What is different about the two choices?

After You’ve Discussed

Note

  • What is actually selected. The “best” embryo is best only in expectation, and the expectation is small. The choice is a probability bet, not a guarantee.
  • What is left out. Most of what shapes a child’s life is environment and chance — neither of which is in the score. Selection promises agency over a slice of the outcome and quiet about the rest.
  • Equity. The service is currently sold at price points that exclude most American families. If it works at all, it works to widen advantage that money already buys.

Key Terms

  • Treatment vs. enhancement — treatment restores normal functioning; enhancement improves a person beyond the normal range. The line matters for insurance, professional duty, and regulation — but all three standard accounts of where to draw it keep collapsing.
  • Normal-function account — Boorse and Daniels’s view that health is the ability to perform what people of your age and sex can typically perform; treatment restores that capacity, enhancement exceeds it.
  • Nosological elasticity — the tendency of disease categories to expand to match demand for the drugs or procedures that treat them, as seen in ADHD diagnosis rates tracking stimulant prescriptions.
  • Somatic vs. germline — somatic edits change one tissue in one person and are not heritable; germline edits change reproductive cells and pass to every descendant. The same molecular tool can do either; the moral weight is in which cells it touches.
  • CRISPR-Cas9 — a programmable gene-editing system that uses a short guide RNA to direct a cutting enzyme (Cas9) to a specific DNA sequence, where it makes a break the cell then repairs, leaving the intended edit in place.
  • Procreative beneficence — Savulescu’s principle that parents have a moral reason to select or engineer the child with the best expected life, applying consequentialist reasoning to both disease risk and ordinary traits.
  • Giftedness (Sandel) — Sandel’s term for the parental disposition to accept a child as she comes, rather than as the product of design; he argues that engineering children corrodes unconditional love and social solidarity.
  • Making vs. growing (Habermas) — Habermas’s distinction between a child who comes into existence through nature’s contingency (and can own her own life) and one who is designed toward a parental plan (and cannot retroactively consent to being someone else’s project).
  • Non-identity problem — Parfit’s puzzle: a person who exists because of a decision that seemed harmful cannot claim to be worse off than she would otherwise have been, because the alternative was that she would never exist at all.
  • Polygenic embryo selection — an IVF add-on that sequences multiple embryos, calculates polygenic risk scores for disease or traits, and lets parents choose which embryo to implant; no editing is performed — only selection among what already exists.
